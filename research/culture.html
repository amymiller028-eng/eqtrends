Are emotions universal?

For decades, the answer seemed to be yes. In the late 1960s, psychologist Paul Ekman showed photos of posed facial expressions to people in the United States, Brazil, Japan and Papua New Guinea. Most of those studies worked a lot like the game above. People looked at a face and chose from a short list of emotion words. Across cultures, they tended to match the same expressions to the same emotions, even in remote villages with little exposure to Western media. Ekman concluded that a handful of basic emotions are hardwired, built into us by evolution.

That idea spread far beyond psychology. It shaped training at the FBI and the TSA, Pixar’s Inside Out and the TV show Lie to Me. And some evidence still supports part of it. In a study of judo athletes at the 2004 Olympic and Paralympic Games, athletes who had been blind since birth made the same spontaneous facial expressions after winning and losing as sighted athletes did. So some of what our faces do may be built in. But making an expression is different from someone else reading it correctly.

Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett and her team took a closer look at how those classic studies were run. They worked with the Himba, a community in Namibia with little contact with Western culture. Instead of handing people a list of emotion words, they gave them 36 photos of posed faces and asked them to sort the photos into piles any way that made sense. Americans who did the same task sorted the faces into the familiar categories, like anger, fear and sadness. The Himba didn’t. They made many more piles and rarely described them with emotion words.

Emotional expression was long believed to be universal. We now know that reading emotions from a face isn’t. In How Emotions Are Made, Barrett argues that emotions aren’t triggered like reflexes. Your brain constructs them, mixing sensations from your body, what’s happening around you and your past experience into its best guess about what you feel. That’s why the same situation doesn’t always lead to the same emotion, and why two people in the same moment can feel completely different things.

In 2019, Barrett and four other scientists reviewed the evidence behind the idea that faces reveal emotions. They didn’t conclude that faces tell us nothing. People do sometimes smile when they’re happy, frown when they’re sad and scowl when they’re angry. But those movements aren’t reliable signatures of an emotion across people, situations and cultures. The same scowl can mean anger, concentration or a headache, and people often feel angry without scowling at all.

Context fills in the rest. In another study, people looked at photos of professional tennis players who had just won or lost a big point. From faces alone, they couldn’t tell winners from losers any better than chance. With the body in view, it was easy. That’s what happened in the photos above.

Culture shapes where you look

Culture can influence more than how people express emotion. Depending on where you live, you may even focus on different parts of the picture when you read someone else. In one study, Japanese and Western participants judged the emotion of a figure at the center of a group. The expressions of the people around the figure swayed the Japanese participants’ judgments more than the Western participants’. Eye tracking showed that the Japanese participants also spent more time looking at the surrounding faces.

That doesn’t mean everyone in a culture reads emotions the same way, or that one approach is better. It means a face seen on its own can leave out part of the picture, and people from different backgrounds may be paying attention to different parts of it.

EQ around the world

Each row is a world region, and each dot is that region’s average overall EQ on our 0–100 scale.

Average EQ varies by about 4 points across regions, from 75.1 in Central America to 70.7 in the Middle East. For comparison, two people picked at random typically differ by about 11 points, so the differences between individuals are much bigger than the differences between regions.

Culture may also shape how people rate themselves. Our assessment asks people to describe their own behavior, and in more collectivist cultures, where modesty and fitting in with the group are valued, people tend to be less self-promoting when they describe themselves. People also judge themselves against the people around them, so a rating of “usually” may not mean the same thing everywhere. Some of the gap between regions may reflect how people rate themselves rather than how they actually behave.

These are patterns among people who took our assessment. They don’t show that culture caused a difference, and a region like Asia isn’t a single culture. A regional average also can’t tell you what a particular colleague feels, how they’ll respond or which EQ skills they need to build. The value of looking at differences isn’t to build a new set of assumptions. It’s to notice where the assumptions we already have may fall short.

We’ve learned that firsthand. In 2005, we tested 3,000 executives in China in their own language, and they scored about 15 points higher than U.S. executives in Self-Management and Relationship Management. At the time, we pointed to a business culture where work relationships are personal and letting others down carries real shame. We wanted to revisit that finding, but we can’t make a fair comparison today. Our assessment now records region rather than country, and today’s test-takers across Asia are a very different group from a set of executives in China twenty years ago.

What it means

Reading people is a skill, not a lookup table. Social Awareness means taking in the whole situation, including what just happened, who else is in the room and how this person tends to show what they feel. That matters most when you work with people whose backgrounds differ from yours, because the cues you rely on may not be the ones they send. Treat your first read as a guess and stay curious. As Dr. Maggie Sass, our Executive Vice President of Content, Research & Professional Services, puts it, good science works by evolving, and so should the way we teach emotional intelligence.

A face is a clue, not an answer. Reading people well takes the whole picture.
