EQ & Generational Differences

Older generations score slightly higher in EQ overall. But each generation has its own strength. Older generations lead in Self-Management, and Gen Z leads in Social Awareness.

What the data shows

Gen ZMillennialsGen XBaby Boomers

  • Overall EQ rises with each older generation, from Gen Z (73.9) to Millennials (74.6), Gen X (75.2) and Baby Boomers (75.6).
  • Self-Management drives the difference, rising 4.1 points from Gen Z (71.9) to Baby Boomers (76.0).
  • Social Awareness runs the other way. Gen Z scores 75.0, the highest of any generation.
  • One in six Gen Z test-takers scores below 65, compared with one in eight Baby Boomers.

In the same role, the gap shrinks

Gen ZMillennialsGen XBaby Boomers

Older test-takers are more likely to hold senior roles, and EQ rises with job level (see EQ & Leader Level). When we compare people at the same level, the generations land much closer together. Among employees, all four generations average between 73.4 and 73.8. Among managers, Gen Z (76.5) and Baby Boomers (76.6) are nearly tied.

Each generation has its own strength

Gen ZBaby Boomers

The skill differences hold up within the same role. Baby Boomers lead in Self-Management at every level, from employees (73.7 vs. 71.6 for Gen Z) to directors (78.4 vs. 75.1). Gen Z leads in Social Awareness at every level. Gen Z managers score 77.1 in Social Awareness, compared with 75.0 for Baby Boomer managers.

Age or generation?

We record age in ten-year bands, so generations are estimated, and six years of data can’t fully separate a generation from a stage of life. One clue points to age. Self-Management climbs with every age band, from 68.5 for people aged 18–19 to 76.1 for people in their 60s. And people in their 20s scored about the same in Self-Management in 2025 (72.2) as in 2021 (71.9), even though Gen Z made up a much larger share of that age group by 2025. That suggests managing your reactions is something people build with time and experience.

What it means

Generational differences in EQ are real but small. Age, gender, job level and the other background details we collect together explain only about 6% of the differences in EQ scores, so knowing someone’s generation tells you very little about their EQ. What it can point to is where colleagues can learn from each other. Younger employees bring a strength in reading people, and more experienced colleagues bring a steadier hand in managing their reactions. Both skills can be developed, and people who retest score an average of 5 points higher.

Source: 689,693 Emotional Intelligence Appraisal self-assessments with a reported age, 2021–2026. Scores are on a 0–100 scale.

Generations are estimated from age band and the year of the assessment: Gen Z (born 1997 or later), Millennials (1981–1996), Gen X (1965–1980) and Baby Boomers (1964 or earlier). The 4.1-point Self-Management gap is about a third of a standard deviation (11.4 points), a small difference.
