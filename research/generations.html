EQ & Generational Differences

Every generation gets a label, and everyone has an opinion about the others. Click an artifact to see how each generation scores on EQ, then see which differences hold up.

Overall EQ by generation

Start with the big picture. Each row is one generation’s average overall EQ score, on our 0–100 scale.

Overall EQ rises a little with each older generation, from 73.9 for Gen Z to 74.6 for Millennials, 75.2 for Gen X and 75.6 for Baby Boomers. That’s less than two points from the youngest generation to the oldest. For comparison, two people picked at random typically differ by about 11 points, so the gap between generations is much smaller than the gap between individuals.

The difference shows up more at the edges. One in six Gen Z test-takers scores below 65, compared with one in eight Baby Boomers. Part of the gap comes from career stage. Older people are more likely to hold senior roles, and when we compare people in the same job, the generations land much closer together (see EQ & Leader Level). To see where the rest comes from, we need to look at the four skills that make up EQ.

Four generations, four skills

EQ is made up of four skills. Each row below is one skill, and each dot is a generation’s average score. The further right, the higher the score. Pick a generation to highlight it.

Two skills tell most of the story. Self-Management, your ability to keep your reactions in check and stay flexible, drives the overall difference. It rises 4.1 points from Gen Z (71.9) to Baby Boomers (76.0), the widest spread of any skill. Self-Awareness follows a similar but smaller pattern.

Social Awareness, picking up on what other people are feeling, runs the other way. Gen Z scores 75.0, the highest of any generation, and Baby Boomers score lowest. Relationship Management is nearly the same across all four generations.

The skill differences hold up within the same role. Baby Boomers lead in Self-Management at every level, from employees (73.7 vs. 71.6 for Gen Z) to directors (78.4 vs. 75.1). Gen Z leads in Social Awareness at every level. Gen Z managers score 77.1 in Social Awareness, compared with 75.0 for Baby Boomer managers.

So even in the same role, the generations bring different strengths. That raises the bigger question of where those differences come from.

Age or generation?

It’s tempting to blame Gen Z’s lower Self-Management on growing up with social media. We heard a version of this in 2009, when Gen Y, today’s millennials, were the youngest people at work and trailed Baby Boomers in Self-Management. The easy explanation then was video games, instant gratification and doting parents. But the data pointed somewhere else. Self-Management rose steadily with age, and that’s still what we see.

We record age in ten-year bands, so generations are estimated, and six years of data can’t fully separate a generation from a stage of life. Self-Management climbs with every age band, from 68.5 for people aged 18–19 to 76.1 for people in their 60s. People in their 20s scored about the same in Self-Management in 2025 (72.2) as in 2021 (71.9), even though Gen Z made up a much larger share of that age group by 2025. And the millennials who trailed in 2009 now sit in the middle, at 73.8. That suggests managing your reactions is something people build with time and experience.

Other research points the same way. A 2025 study of 1,525 postgraduate students found no significant overall EQ differences across age groups, but found differences in specific skills. Older participants reported greater emotional clarity and a better ability to repair a bad mood, while younger participants reported paying more attention to their own emotions. The study compared age groups. It did not establish distinct generational traits.

In the “OK Boomer” chapter of Algospeak, Adam Aleksic explores the skepticism surrounding generational labels. The underlying question matters for EQ. When younger and older people score differently, are we seeing a generational signature, or differences in age, experience and life stage? Our results give us reason to be cautious about the labels. Overall EQ differences shrink substantially when we compare people at the same job level. Differences in specific skills remain, but our data cannot establish whether they reflect generation, age or accumulated experience.

What it means

The EQ differences between these age-based groups are small. They do not establish that generations have distinct emotional-intelligence profiles. Age, gender, job level and the other background details we collect together explain only about 6% of the differences in EQ scores, so knowing someone’s generation tells you very little about their EQ. What it can point to is where colleagues can learn from each other. Younger employees bring a strength in reading people, and more experienced colleagues bring a steadier hand in managing their reactions.

Self-Management grows with experience, but you don’t have to wait decades for it. Practice speeds it up. In our data, people who retest score an average of 5 points higher.
