EQ & Hard Times

In 2008 we found that collective EQ had been climbing for years, until the recession hit. COVID gave us a chance to look again.

2003–2008

Our first look: collective EQ and the 2008 recession

At the end of 2008, we looked at how the collective EQ of the U.S. workforce had changed since 2003. This study first appeared in Emotional Intelligence 2.0 (2009).

  • Average EQ rose about four points between 2003 and 2007.
  • The share of people with high EQ skills rose from 13.7% in 2003 to 18.3% in 2007, and the share with low EQ skills fell from 31.0% to 14.0%.
  • Scores rose even among people who had never been tested or trained before.
  • In 2008, collective EQ dropped for the first time since tracking began. The share with high EQ skills fell from 18.3% to 16.7%.

How we explained it at the time

The authors pointed to the recession that began in December 2007. In their view, hard times create intense, lasting negative emotions and stress, and stress uses up the mental resources people need to stay calm and collected:

“For many people, their EQ skills desert them at precisely the time when they need these skills the most—under stress.”

They also saw the rise among people who had never been trained as a sign that EQ is contagious: people who practice emotionally intelligent behaviors may pass them on to the people around them. Are emotions contagious? See the research →

So what does EQ look like through hard times now?

2020

What did COVID do to EQ?

COVID was arguably as big a shock to working life as the 2008 recession, so we looked for the same kind of dip. We found one. It was smaller than in 2008, and it passed within a few months.

  • In April 2020, 21.8% of people scored low, compared with about 18% in a typical April. Fewer people scored high.
  • All four EQ skills dropped. Self-Management, keeping your reactions in check, fell the most.
  • Scores stayed a little lower through July and were back to normal by August.
  • The dip was unique to 2020: from 2017 to 2019, April scores barely moved.

A typical year is the average of the same month in 2017–2019. Fewer people took the assessment in spring 2020, so part of the dip may reflect who was taking it.
