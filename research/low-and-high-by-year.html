EQ & Hard Times

Since 2003, we’ve tracked how many people score high and low in emotional intelligence and looked at those patterns alongside what was happening in the world at the time. Our first finding, published in 2009, was that the share of people with high EQ skills dropped for the first time in 2008, the year the U.S. recession took hold. Travel through 23 years of EQ data to see what else we’ve found about high and low scores and the context around them.

What it means for you

EQ is easy when life is easy.

More than twenty years of data tell the same story: hard times show up in EQ scores, and they pass. Here’s how to be one of the people who hold steady.

1

Expect the slip.

Hard times use up the mental energy EQ runs on. If you catch yourself snapping or shutting down, you haven’t lost your skills. It’s a signal to slow down.

2

Feel it. Don’t follow it.

Tense times don’t give anyone a pass on how they treat people. You can see clearly how hard things are, notice the emotion, and still choose what you do next.

3

Practice while the skies are clear.

The skills that hold up under stress are the ones you’ve practiced until they’re second nature. Build them on ordinary days, so they’re there on the hard ones.

4

Be someone’s guidepost.

When you stay steady, the people around you notice, and moods spread. Are emotions contagious? →

* How we turn percentages into millions of people

To make the numbers easier to picture, we apply our test-takers’ percentages to the U.S. workforce, as Travis Bradberry and Jean Greaves did in 2009. For 2003–2008 we use their published figures, based on a workforce of 180 million. From 2010 on we use 163 million, the number of people employed in the United States according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (Employment Situation, Table A-1). These numbers are estimates, not head counts.

2003–2008: Bradberry, T. & Greaves, J. (2009). Emotional Intelligence 2.0. TalentSmart. Adapted as “Collective EQ: A Case Study” (TalentSmartEQ, 2022). U.S. test-takers.

2010–2026: Emotional Intelligence Appraisal self-assessments, 1,877,412 in all. Scores are on a 0–100 scale; low is below 65 and high is above 85, the same cutoffs every year. 2026 runs through September and covers 71,173 assessments. The 2003–2008 study used its own scoring and cutoffs, so its percentages can’t be compared directly with later years. Its average EQ figures were published as a chart without values.
